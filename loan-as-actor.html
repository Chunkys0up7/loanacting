01 — The Problem

Every agentic mortgage platform is still station-based

The mortgage industry's "AI transformation" has swapped humans for agents at each workstation — but left the assembly line itself unchanged.

From Tavant's MAYA to AWS Bedrock Agents to UiPath Maestro, every current approach follows the same pattern: a supervisor or orchestrator agent sits at the top, delegating tasks to specialized sub-agents (document extraction, compliance checking, underwriting analysis) that act on a passive loan record. The loan remains a row in a database — acted upon, never acting.

This means every transition between stages (processing → underwriting → closing → servicing) is a seam where context drops, state gets lost, and exceptions get stuck in queues. The agentic SaaS vendors have automated the stations, but not the handoffs. And in mortgage, the handoffs are where everything breaks.

The station assembly line — what exists today
Station-based (current)
  • Loan is a passive data record acted upon by external agents
  • Handoffs between stages lose context and create queue friction
  • Exception handling requires human routing to the right specialist
  • Audit trail is scattered across multiple systems and logs
  • Secondary market: loan is a static snapshot exported from LOS
Loan-as-Actor (proposed)
  • Loan is an autonomous entity with goals, state, and reasoning
  • No handoffs — the loan carries its context and recruits capability
  • Loan self-detects exceptions and spawns resolution sub-agents
  • Single cognitive diary captures every event, decision, and rationale
  • Secondary market: loan carries its complete provenance and history
02 — The Inversion

The loan is already the protagonist

A mortgage loan has a legal identity, a 30-year lifespan, and it gets bought and sold as an asset. It's already the protagonist of its own story. We propose making it the protagonist computationally.

Instead of agents executing on a loan, the loan executes agents. It doesn't get handed off — it reaches out and grabs what it needs.

S

Self-aware state

The loan knows its own FICO, LTV, product type, lock expiration, and outstanding conditions. It reasons about what it needs next.

D

Demand-driven capability

When the loan needs a credit pull, it spawns a credit sub-agent. When it needs an appraisal, it spawns an appraisal sub-agent. The loan doesn't care if it's an internal service or an API vendor.

R

Self-healing resilience

If a sub-agent fails (vendor timeout, data mismatch), the loan's supervision logic kicks in — retry, escalate, try an alternative, alert a human. No orchestrator bottleneck.

P

Portable identity

When sold to a servicer, the loan doesn't export a static snapshot. It hands over its complete diary, and the new owner spins up a fresh actor that reconstitutes with full history.

Ten structural advantages

01

Ontological inversion

Vesta organizes agents around its workflow engine. MOZAIQ organizes agents by function (income, assets, compliance). Loan-as-Actor organizes computation around the entity that matters — the loan itself. This isn't a better LOS. It's a different category: the loan has goals, makes decisions, and recruits capability on demand.

02

Zero-handoff lifecycle

Despite every advance in the space — Vesta's modern LOS, Valon's modern MSP — the origination-to-servicing boundary is still a data transfer between two systems. The loan gets a new identity in each. With Loan-as-Actor, there is no transfer. The same actor continues through origination, servicing, modification, and disposition. Context is never lost because it was never handed off.

03

Self-healing without bottleneck

Orchestrator-based systems create a single point of failure. When exceptions pile up at MOZAIQ's swarm coordinator or Vesta's workflow engine, the orchestrator becomes the constraint. Loan-actors use OTP supervision trees — each loan handles its own exceptions independently, with automatic restart on failure. The system degrades gracefully under load.

04

Unified cognitive audit trail

Even the best current platforms scatter decision data across per-agent or per-module logs. A regulator asking "why was this loan approved?" must reconstruct the story. The loan's diary captures every event, every decision, and every piece of reasoning in a single append-only log — with compacted views for regulators, underwriters, and secondary market analysts.

05

Hot-swappable regulatory compliance

When Fannie Mae updates the Selling Guide, current platforms require configuration updates (Vesta), module redeployment (Valon), or agent retraining (MOZAIQ). The Actor Model supports hot code loading — new compliance rules deploy while loan-actors are running. Existing loans pick up new rules on their next message cycle without restart or downtime.

06

Portable identity for secondary market

Today, selling a loan means exporting a static MISMO data tape — a snapshot that loses the full context of how the loan reached its current state. Every platform — legacy or modern — produces the same static export. A loan-actor transfers its diary: complete provenance, decision trail, and compliance history. The buyer reconstitutes the actor with full fidelity.

07

Push-model pooling and trading

No current platform — not Vesta, not Valon, not MOZAIQ — enables loans to actively signal their eligibility for investor pools. Loan-as-Actor transforms the secondary market from a pull model (capital markets desk queries a database) to a push model (loans evaluate investor criteria and advertise their eligibility). This is a net-new capability that doesn't exist anywhere.

08

Data security by architecture, not by policy

Station-based systems replicate the complete loan record across every platform that touches it — LOS, doc management, underwriting engine, servicing system, secondary marketing tools. Each copy is an attack surface. The actor model inverts this: data stays with the loan. Sub-agents receive only the fields they need (a credit-pull agent gets the SSN, not the property address). Actor isolation means no shared memory and no shared database — compromising one loan doesn't give lateral access to others. The append-only diary with chain anchors is tamper-evident by design. And right-to-deletion (GLBA/CCPA) becomes straightforward: destroy one actor and its diary, not hunt through a dozen systems for every copy of a borrower's data.

09

Vendor-portable operating knowledge

ICE's 70% market share depends on a lender's operating logic being stuck inside Encompass — business rules in a GUI-configured engine, custom fields in a proprietary schema, workflows that take a multi-year IT project to migrate out. Vesta's story depends on the same stickiness, just with fresher infrastructure. Loan-as-Actor moves the operating manual into a git repo of Markdown — the knowledge vault — that any compliant runtime can read. Switching from one actor runtime to another is a configuration change, not a re-implementation. The perimeter only works if the lender's operations live inside it.

10

Regulator-readable by default

Today, a CFPB or state exam is a forensic reconstruction project across LOS, servicing, imaging, and compliance systems — each with its own field identifiers and log formats. With loan-as-actor: the vault is the documented shop procedure, the diary is the narrated decision log, and the chain anchors prove neither was altered after the fact. An exam becomes a same-day replay: "show me this loan's diary and the matching vault procedure." The structural question "did this loan follow your written process?" becomes answerable by running a test, not by assembling a forensic file.

04 — Theoretical Foundations

The Actor Model meets mortgage

Three well-established paradigms converge: Carl Hewitt's Actor Model (1973), Event Sourcing, and Intelligent Digital Twins from Industry 4.0.

The Erlang origin story

Erlang was created at Ericsson in the late 1980s to run telephone switches — systems managing millions of concurrent calls, any of which could fail at any moment, without ever taking the system down. The insight: model each call as a tiny, isolated process with its own state. Processes communicate only by sending messages. If one crashes, restart it. No other process is affected.

This maps to mortgage with uncanny precision. A telephone switch manages 100,000 concurrent calls. A large lender manages 100,000 concurrent loans. Both are massive concurrency of independent, long-running, stateful entities where partial failures are the norm, not the exception.

Actor model — core properties

OTP supervision trees

OTP's killer feature is supervision. Supervisors are processes whose only job is monitoring other processes and restarting them on failure. They form a tree: at the top, an application supervisor. Below it, portfolio supervisors (maybe one per product type). Below those, individual loan actors.

When a loan actor crashes — a sub-agent returns corrupted data, a message handler throws an exception — the supervisor automatically restarts it. Because the loan's state is persisted in the diary (the append-only event log), the restarted actor replays its diary and picks up exactly where it left off. This is the "let it crash" philosophy: write clean happy-path code, and let the infrastructure handle recovery.

The three-loop harness — what makes it an agent, not a record

A naive actor implementation is purely reactive: a message arrives, the actor processes it, returns to waiting. That gives you a smart database. For the loan to be genuinely agentic, it runs three interleaved execution loops simultaneously — each serving a distinct purpose.

R

Reactive processing

Standard GenServer message handling. Sub-agent results, external webhooks, timer expirations arrive in the mailbox and get processed sequentially. This is the baseline every actor system has.

♡

Periodic reflection (heartbeat)

Every 30 seconds, the loan wakes itself up and evaluates its situation across multiple dimensions — stuck sub-agents, upcoming deadlines, aging conditions, missing capabilities, stale state. Most reflections conclude "no concerns." That's the point — the heartbeat is always running.

→

State-driven planning

On every lifecycle transition, the loan consults its own knowledge of what the new state requires and spawns what's missing. No orchestrator tells it what to do next. The loan plans its own work based on where it is.

These three loops together are what distinguish an agent from a reactive record. The heartbeat is what lets the loan notice things that didn't happen — the appraisal that hasn't come back in 5 days, the lock that's approaching expiration, the condition that's been outstanding without action. The planning loop is what lets the loan drive its own lifecycle without external prompting. Both must be foundational, not bolted on — a loan missing either loop silently degrades to a reactive state machine.

Deterministic-first, LLM-escalated

The loan uses plain rules where the answer is unambiguous and LLM reasoning where judgment is required. This boundary is deliberate — LLM cost, latency, and nondeterminism all compound if invoked loosely.

Always deterministic

State transitions, LTV/DTI math, condition matching, timer arithmetic, chain anchor hashing, heartbeat dimension checks. Rules with unambiguous answers.

Escalated to LLM

When deterministic checks surface a concern that requires judgment — how to respond to a low appraisal, whether to investigate or deny on income discrepancy, when to extend a rate lock.

Always LLM

Multi-option decisions with context-dependent correct answers, diary compaction into working memory, natural language generation for borrower-facing notifications.

Every LLM call writes both its full trace (prompt, response, tokens, latency) to a separate trace log and its structured decision to the diary. The trace log captures how the LLM got there; the diary captures what the loan decided. Together they provide complete reproducibility for any decision.

06 — The Cognitive Diary

An immutable record of everything

The diary is both the loan's audit trail and its working memory — a single append-only log that captures events, reasoning, actions, and decisions with timestamps and tags.

Traditional event sourcing captures facts: "appraisal received at T1 with value $X." The cognitive diary adds the loan's reasoning — why it chose vendor A over vendor B, why it escalated, what its confidence level was. This is the difference between a regulatory audit trail and a cognitive audit trail.

/loan-1247/
diary.jsonl             ← immutable, append-only ground truth
summary.md            ← compacted current state (agent's working memory)
decisions.md          ← every decision point with rationale
conditions.md         ← outstanding / cleared conditions
tools-used.md         ← sub-agent invocation history
mismo-manifest.xml   ← MISMO-standard document registry
/documents/           ← e-folder (actual artifacts)
/application/  /credit/  /title/  /appraisal/  /closing/
/servicing/            ← post-close lifecycle data
/chain-anchors/       ← on-chain proof hashes at checkpoints

Time travel

Reconstruct the loan's state at any historical moment by replaying the diary to that timestamp. Examiners can see exactly what was known, decided, and done at any point.

Context compaction

The compacted files (summary.md, decisions.md) are the loan-agent's "attention layer." It reads summary.md for context rather than replaying its entire 6-month diary — like an LLM reading a compacted context window.

Portable identity

When sold, the loan hands over its diary. The new servicer spins up an actor, feeds it the diary, and it reconstitutes with complete history. Radically simpler than today's MISMO XML transfer snapshots.

Chain anchors

At key checkpoints (funded, first payment, sold), hash the diary and write it to a chain. Buyers verify provenance without accessing sensitive borrower data. Immutability without on-chain PII.

07 — Knowledge as Code

Operating procedures the agent can read

The diary captures what the loan did. The vault captures what the loan is supposed to do, and why. Both are Markdown. Both are git-backed. Both are human-readable and agent-retrievable. Traditional platforms hide operating logic in configured workflows; loan-as-actor makes it docs-as-code.

In Encompass, the rule that says "if the appraisal comes in more than 10% below purchase, route to the underwriting manager" lives in the Business Rules engine — a configured, proprietary, screenshot-documented decision. When regulators ask why a particular loan was handled a particular way, someone exports a PDF of the rule graph and hopes the field identifiers still make sense. In Vesta, the same rule lives in a workflow JSON payload inside the platform's system of record — portable in theory, opaque in practice.

In loan-as-actor, that rule is a Markdown file under vault/procedures/ with YAML frontmatter declaring when an agent should use it. The loan retrieves it by description at runtime. A new compliance officer reviews it in a pull request. An examiner reads it as prose.

/vault/
VAULT.md             ← agent entry point; three-hop retrieval map
CHANGELOG.md          ← drift log for stable procedures
procedures/        ← runbooks — "use when a rate lock is within 5 days of expiry"
tools/              ← interface specs: AUS, credit, AVM, eRecording, etc.
reference/          ← concepts, schemas, state-machine invariants
adr/                ← dated architecture decisions, immutable once accepted
tests/               ← input → expected-output pairs; self-check after a procedure
anti-patterns/    ← "don't do X; do Y instead"
_meta/             ← conventions, note templates, Dataview health queries

Every note declares its own trigger

The single most important field in a vault note is the description: line — written as a trigger the agent matches against, not a summary. This is the retrieval handle:

---
type: procedure
name: Refresh Rate Sheet Cache
description: "Use when downstream pricing looks stale or a lender
              publishes an off-cycle rate sheet update outside the
              normal morning refresh window."
last_verified: 2026-04-22
owner: CK
status: stable
preconditions:
  - No active pricing-lock session in progress
tools_required:
  - [[tools/lender-portal-api]]
---
V

Version-controlled operating logic

Every change to a procedure is a git commit with a reviewer. Compare with Encompass Business Rules, which live in a proprietary database edited via dropdowns. Diff two versions of a runbook. Bisect a regression. Revert a bad change. Write tests against the procedure.

R

Regulator-readable

An examiner doesn't need a guided demo of the Business Rules configuration. They read vault/procedures/appraisal-dispute.md to understand how the shop handles a low appraisal, then replay the loan's diary to confirm that's what actually happened.

T

Test-linked procedures

Each procedure links to a test under vault/tests/ with an explicit input → expected output. Did the procedure still work after last Tuesday's change? Run the test. The procedure and its proof live together, in git, in one repo.

A

Agent retrieval, three hops

Agent starts at VAULT.md, narrows to a folder INDEX, retrieves the specific note. Every note is under ~400 lines and declares its preconditions, inputs, outputs, and tools required as structured frontmatter. No prompt-engineering. No fine-tuning. No opaque model weights.

The workflow engine is the wrong abstraction. The workflow is prose. The engine is a programming language that happens to render as dropdowns.

Why this dismantles the ICE moat

ICE's market position depends on the operating logic of a lender being stuck inside the ICE platform. Business rules, workflow configurations, custom fields, screen layouts — all living in Encompass-proprietary formats that make switching a multi-year IT project. When a lender's knowledge base is a git repo of Markdown files, the switch becomes: point the new runtime at the same vault. The loan-actor retrieves the same procedures. The same audit trail structure applies. The same test suite validates. The moat drains to zero.

This is how a serious challenger beats a 70%-market-share incumbent: not by building a better workflow engine, but by moving the workflow out of the engine entirely.

09 — At Scale

From 1 loan to 100,000

The BEAM VM (Erlang's runtime) was designed to run millions of lightweight processes. Modern implementations extend this across languages and cloud platforms.

A bare Erlang process starts at about 2KB of memory — but a Loan Actor carrying borrower data, property info, loan terms, conditions, and compacted working memory is realistically 50–200KB when hydrated. Even so, 50,000 active loans at 200KB is roughly 10GB — comfortable on a single node. And with virtual actors (Orleans-style), most loans are dehydrated at any given moment: a loan in quiet servicing consumes zero memory until its next event. The BEAM VM supports transparent distribution across multiple nodes: a message to {:loan, "1247"} works whether Loan 1247 lives on the local machine or across the cluster.

The runtime bet: BEAM (Erlang / Elixir)

The prototype is built on the BEAM VM — Erlang's runtime, 30+ years of battle-testing in telecom, Ericsson's answer to "how do you run millions of independent stateful processes where any one can crash without taking the system down." The mortgage problem is structurally the same: long-running, independent, stateful, failure-tolerant. Elixir gives it modern ergonomics; OTP supervision gives it the failure story out of the box; hot code loading lets regulatory updates deploy while live loans keep running.

The actor pattern also lands elsewhere: Microsoft Orleans brings Virtual Actors to the .NET world (a loan in quiet servicing rehydrates on the next payment event), Cloudflare Durable Objects productize actors at the edge, and Akka ports OTP's supervision model to the JVM — used at scale by LinkedIn, PayPal, and several banks. Those are viable targets for future ports or specific sub-systems; the primary architecture assumes BEAM.

Virtual actor lifecycle

You don't manually manage 500,000 loan-actor instances. The runtime activates an actor when a message arrives, loads its state from the diary, processes the message, writes to the diary, and deactivates when idle. At any given moment, only the loans with active events are resident in memory. A loan in quiet servicing consumes zero compute until its next payment event.

10 — Secondary Market

Loans that sell themselves

A loan-actor that carries its own provenance, characteristics, and compliance history can actively participate in pooling and trading.

Instead of a capital markets desk running queries against a database to find eligible assets, the loans themselves respond to investor appetite signals. A loan-actor can evaluate: "I'm a 30-year fixed, 720 FICO, 75% LTV, fully documented, I satisfy the criteria for Pool X." This transforms the secondary market from a pull model (desk queries loans) to a push model (loans advertise their eligibility).

The loan carries its complete cognitive provenance. When a buyer validates a pool, they don't rely on a static data tape — they verify each loan's diary against its chain anchor.

The MISMO + chain story

MISMO already defines the canonical data model for mortgage documents. The diary folder aligns with MISMO document categories while adding the cognitive layer. The chain anchors provide immutability proof without putting PII on-chain. Together, you get: MISMO for interoperability, the diary for provenance, and the chain for trust.
